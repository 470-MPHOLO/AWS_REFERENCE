1. How research actually works

1.1 Research is a conversation, not a report

The single most useful idea in this document is this: research is a conversation that has been going on for decades, and your job is to join it, not to restart it. Every field already contains people who have asked your question, disagreed with each other, built on each other's work and left gaps. A research report is your contribution to that conversation: here is what is known, here is where the accounts disagree, here is the gap, here is what I did about it, here is what it means.

This reframes everything. A literature review is not a list of summaries. It is a map of who says what, where they disagree, what the evidence supports, and where you stand. A citation is not decoration; it is a pointer to the person you are responding to. When you understand this, referencing stops feeling like a chore and starts feeling like the mechanism of the whole enterprise.

1.2 The research cycle

StageWhat happensWhat you produce
1. QuestionA problem is identified, narrowed and made answerableA research question or hypothesis
2. LiteratureRead what is known, find the disagreement and the gapA literature review
3. MethodDecide how the question can be answered and what would count as evidenceA method section, or a design
4. DataCollect measurements, observations, cases, artefacts, survey responsesA dataset
5. AnalysisApply the analysis method and interpret what the numbers or evidence meanResults and discussion
6. ClaimState what you conclude and how confident you areA conclusion with hedged, defensible claims
7. Peer reviewIndependent experts check method and claims before publicationReviewer comments, revisions
8. PublicationWork enters the record and can be citedA paper, standard, report or thesis
9. Critique and replicationOthers test, extend or dispute itNew papers citing, confirming or refuting yours

Two things follow from this cycle. First, published work is not automatically true; it is the best current candidate that survived scrutiny. Second, a single paper is never the whole story. This is why a good report cites several sources agreeing or disagreeing, and not one paper that happens to support the conclusion you wanted.

1.3 Primary, secondary and tertiary sources

TypeWhat it isSTEM examplesWhen to use it
PrimaryOriginal work, first-hand data, the thing itselfA randomised trial, a measurement dataset, the IEEE standard text, the source code, the lab notebook, a patent, a census fileWhenever you can. Strongest evidence for a specific claim.
SecondaryAnalysis, summary or interpretation of primary workA review article, a textbook, a systematic review, a technical white paper summarising researchTo orient yourself quickly, and to cite a synthesis of many studies.
TertiaryCompiled reference materialEncyclopaedias, dictionaries, Wikipedia, handbooks of constantsTo learn vocabulary and find primary sources. Rarely cited directly in STEM.
The practical rule. Use tertiary sources to orient yourself, secondary sources to understand the shape of the field, and primary sources to make your own claims. A report that cites only textbooks is a report that has not engaged with the literature.

1.4 Peer review: what it does and does not guarantee

Peer review means that independent experts in the field assessed the work before publication. It filters obvious errors, methodological weaknesses and unsupported claims. It does not guarantee that the findings are correct, that the statistics are sound, that the code works, or that the authors have no conflicts of interest. Retractions of peer-reviewed papers happen every year. Peer review raises the base rate of quality; it does not certify truth.

This is why you evaluate sources yourself (Part 2) rather than assuming that the word "journal" settles it. It also explains why preprints exist and why you must flag them: a preprint has not been peer reviewed, so it is a claim, not a finding.

1.5 How a paper is structured: IMRaD

Most STEM papers follow IMRaD: Introduction, Methods, Results, and Discussion. Knowing the structure changes how you read. The abstract tells you what the paper claims. The introduction tells you what gap it addresses. The methods tell you whether you should believe the results. The results are the data, not the interpretation. The discussion is where the authors interpret, admit limitations and say what should come next.

The reading method that saves hours. Read in this order: title, abstract, conclusion, figures and tables, introduction, results, methods. Most papers can be dismissed after the abstract and conclusion. Only papers that survive that filter deserve a full read. When you do read for detail, read the methods before the results, because a result is only as good as the method that produced it.

1.6 The literature review as a map

A literature review answers four questions. What is agreed? What is disputed? What is missing? Where does my work sit? Organise by theme or debate, never by source. A review that says "Smith found X. Jones found Y. Patel found Z." is a list. A review that says "The field agrees that X holds under condition A, but Smith and Jones disagree on condition B, and neither addresses small deployments, which is the gap this report fills" is a review.

Weak organisationStrong organisation
Author by author, in order of publicationTheme by theme, with authors grouped by position
Describes each sourceCompares and contrasts sources
No gap identifiedA clear gap that the study addresses
No judgementEvidence weighed, quality assessed, contradictions surfaced

1.7 Note-taking that saves you later

Every time you read something useful, capture five things in your notes immediately: the full citation, the page number, the exact claim or quote, your own one-line comment on why it matters, and the tag that links it to a theme in your outline. If you capture the citation at the moment of reading, the reference list writes itself. If you do not, you will spend the last night hunting for a page number and will be tempted to guess, which is how fabricated references happen.

2. What counts as a good source

2.1 The hierarchy of evidence

Not all sources are equal. In STEM the rough hierarchy, strongest to weakest, is:

RankSource typeNotes
1Systematic reviews and meta-analysesSynthesise many studies; strongest for "what does the evidence say overall"
2Peer-reviewed journal articles and top-tier conference papersThe backbone of a STEM literature review
3Standards (IEEE, ISO, IEC, TIA, NIST)Authoritative for specifications, protocols, measurement and compliance
4Government and institutional reports, official statisticsAuthoritative for policy, national data, regulations
5Industry white papers from reputable vendorsUseful for engineering detail and market context; commercially motivated, say so
6Textbooks and edited handbooksGood for established theory; slower to reflect new work
7Vendor documentation and release notesCorrect for what a product does; not evidence about the field
8Reputable technical websites and magazinesUseful for orientation; cite sparingly
9News articlesUse only for the fact that something happened, never for the science
10Blogs, forums, Wikipedia, AI chatbotsNot citable as evidence in academic work. Use to orient, then cite the primary source they point to.

2.2 The six-question source test

Before you cite anything, ask six questions. If a source fails two or more, find another.

QuestionWhat to checkRed flag
1. Who wrote it?Named authors, institutional affiliation, relevant expertiseNo author, no affiliation, or an author with no traceable record
2. Where was it published?Recognised journal, conference, standards body, government departmentUnknown publisher, journal with an implausibly broad scope
3. Is it current?Publication date appropriate to the type (Part 3)Latest data from a decade ago presented as current
4. Is it peer reviewed?Stated on the journal site, or the paper is clearly a preprintPreprint presented as if peer reviewed
5. Is it supported?Claims backed by data, method described, references givenBold claims, no method, no data, no references
6. Why does it exist?Academic, regulatory, or commercial purpose statedVendor material presented as neutral evidence

2.3 Predatory journals: how to spot them

Predatory journals charge a publication fee and provide no real peer review. They exist because publication is a currency in academia. Citing one is not automatically misconduct, but it weakens your evidence and a marker who knows the field will notice.

Warning signWhat you see
Implausible speedAcceptance within days, including for a full experimental paper
Flattery by emailUnsolicited invitations to submit, addressed to "Dear esteemed researcher"
No real editorial boardBoard page missing, or names listed without consent
Fake or meaningless metrics"Impact factor" from an organisation you cannot verify
Scope too broadA journal covering "all engineering and science"
No retraction policyNo visible ethics, corrections or retraction page

Citation chaining. Find one good recent paper on your topic. Read its reference list (backwards in time) and use "cited by" to find papers that cite it (forwards in time). Repeat for two or three seed papers. Within an hour you will have the shape of the field, including the seminal older work and the current frontier. This is faster and better than keyword searching alone.

3. Reference dates: when old is fine and when it is fatal

3.1 The principle

An old source is not automatically a bad source. Age is a signal, not a verdict. What matters is whether the claim you are using it for is still valid. A 1948 paper by Shannon is still the correct source for information theory. A 2015 paper on the throughput of 802.11ac is now superseded for current design decisions but still valid as a historical benchmark. The question is always: has this claim been superseded, and does my reader need the current version?

3.2 Acceptable age by source type

Source typeTypical acceptable ageWhy
Seminal or foundational workAny ageIt is the origin of the idea; you cite it because it is the origin, not because it is current
Standards (IEEE, ISO, IEC)Cite the edition in force; check for amendmentsThe year is part of the identity. A superseded standard is a wrong citation.
Method or algorithmUsually within 5 years for fast-moving fields; older if still the accepted methodIf a newer method dominates, using the old one needs justification
TextbooksLatest edition preferred; older edition acceptable if you cite that edition and say soNew editions correct errors and add developments
Technology performance figures2 to 5 years, often lessCapabilities change quickly; a 2016 figure for Wi-Fi 6 is impossible because the standard did not exist
Statistics and market dataThe latest release onlySuperseded statistics are a factual error
Government policy and regulationCite the version currently in forcePolicy changes; an old version misstates the law
Review articles5 to 10 years, depending on the field's paceReviews age faster than the primary work they summarise
Conference papersField dependent; computing moves fast, civil engineering slowlyJudge by whether the finding is still the state of the art

3.3 The citation window

Many journals and examiners use a rule of thumb: in a literature review, a substantial proportion of your sources should be recent, often at least half within the last five years. This is not a law; it is a signal that you have engaged with the current frontier and not just with whatever came up first in a search. A report whose newest source is from 2016, submitted in 2026, reads as though you stopped reading halfway. A report whose sources are all from last year reads as though you do not know where the ideas came from.

The balanced shape. A strong reference list has three layers. Foundational sources that establish the concepts, which may be decades old. Method and standards sources that are current within the relevant cycle. Recent sources from the last few years that show you know the current state of the art. If you can point to all three layers, you have done the reading.

3.4 When using an old source is a mistake

SituationWhy it is a mistakeWhat to do
Citing a superseded standardThe specification has changed; your design may be wrongCheck the standards body for the current edition and any amendments
Presenting old statistics as currentFactually wrong at submissionUse the latest release, and state the reference year
Describing a technology using a pre-standard sourceThe final standard may differ from the draftCite the final standard
Using an old method when a better one is standardYour results are not comparable with current workUse the current method, or justify the older one explicitly
Citing an old paper for a claim that has since been retracted or overturnedThe claim is no longer supportedCheck for retraction and for later refutations before citing

3.5 When using only new sources is a mistake

The opposite failure is equally common and less often noticed. If every source is from the last two years, you have read the surface of the field and not its foundations. You will not know why the current method exists, what it replaced, or what problem it was invented to solve. You will also be more likely to repeat a mistake the field already corrected ten years ago. Foundational sources earn their place in a reference list.

3.6 How to check whether a source is still valid

  1. Check the publication date and ask what has changed in the field since then.
  2. Search for the paper's title to see whether it has been retracted, corrected or heavily criticised. Publisher sites and Retraction Watch are both useful.
  3. Look at who cites it. If the only citations are from the same authors, it may not be influential.
  4. Check whether a newer edition, revision or superseding standard exists.
  5. For standards, check the issuing body's site for the current edition and any amendments.

4. Harvard referencing, in detail

4.1 Why we reference

Referencing does four jobs. It credits the person whose work you used. It allows verification, so a reader can check whether you have represented the source fairly. It shows the evidence base for your claims. And it separates your contribution from everyone else's, which is the foundation of academic integrity. A reference list is not a formality; it is the audit trail of your argument.

4.2 The two halves

HalfWhere it goesWhat it does
In-text citationAt the point in your sentence where the claim is madePoints the reader to the full entry in the reference list
Reference listAt the end, alphabetical by author surnameGives the full details needed to find the source
One rule that catches most students. Every in-text citation must have a matching entry in the reference list, and every entry in the reference list must be cited at least once in the text. Nothing in, nothing out. If those two sets do not match exactly, marks are lost regardless of how good the prose is.

4.3 In-text citation forms

Author as subjectSurname (year) arguesNIST (2017) recommends against forced periodic password changes.
Direct quotation(Surname, year, p. x)Wi-Fi 6 "splits each channel into resource units" (IEEE, 2021, p. 43).
Two authors(Surname and Surname, year)(Smith and Jones, 2020)
Three or more authors(First author et al., year)(Smith et al., 2020)
Corporate author(Organisation, year)(World Health Organization, 2024)
No date(Surname, no date)(Smith, no date)
Several sources, one claimSeparated by semicolons, alphabetical(Jones, 2021; NIST, 2017; Smith, 2020)
Source quoted in another source(Original, year, cited in Secondary, year)(Shannon, 1948, cited in Stallings, 2020)
Same author, same year, two worksAdd a, b, c(Smith, 2020a) and (Smith, 2020b)
Secondary citation. Use "cited in" only when you genuinely cannot access the original. It signals that you have not read the primary source, which is a weakness. If the original is available, read it and cite it directly.

4.5 The rules that are easiest to get wrong

The cardinal sin of referencing. A reference that does not exist is worse than a missing reference, because it is academic misconduct. This includes references generated by an AI tool that look plausible but point to nothing. Every entry must be a source you have actually opened. Verify the DOI, verify the page number, verify the year. Part 7 covers this in detail.

4.6 Managing references without pain

Use a reference manager: Zotero, Mendeley or EndNote. Install the browser connector, and capture the citation the moment you read the source. The manager stores the metadata, formats the reference in Harvard and inserts in-text citations into your word processor. This removes the two failure modes that cost students most marks: a reference list that does not match the citations, and a page number guessed at midnight. A reference manager does not do the intellectual work, but it removes the clerical errors that hide good work.

5. Writing well in STEM: plain language and lean paragraphs

5.1 The principle

Academic writing is not complicated writing. It is precise writing. The goal is that a competent reader in your field understands your claim on the first read, and can check it. Complexity that does not add precision is a defect, not a virtue. The best technical writing is plain, short and specific.

WEAKER

It is important to note that in the context of the deployment of wireless networking infrastructure within an institutional environment, a considerable number of factors must be taken into consideration with regard to the optimisation of performance outcomes.

STRONGER

Campus wireless performance depends on three factors: access point density, channel planning and client behaviour.

The second version is shorter, names the three factors, and makes a checkable claim. The first version says nothing a reader can use.

5.2 Sentence-level rules

RuleWhyExample
Average 15 to 25 words per sentenceLonger sentences lose the reader before the verb arrivesSplit at "and", "which" and "because" if the sentence exceeds 30 words
One idea per sentenceTwo ideas in one sentence produce ambiguity about which is the claim"The AP failed, so the building lost coverage" beats "The AP, which had been installed three years earlier and was due for replacement, failed and caused coverage loss in the building."
Active voice by defaultNames the actor and shortens the sentence"The sweep marks expired permits" not "Expired permits are marked by the sweep"
Passive only when the actor is unknown or irrelevantPassive hides responsibility and adds words"The cable was cut during construction" is correct passive; "It has been decided" is evasion
Cut nominalisationsVerbs turned into nouns add words and lose force"We analysed" not "We conducted an analysis of"
Define acronyms at first useNot every reader shares your shorthand"Wireless local area network (WLAN)" then "WLAN" thereafter
Hedge claims to match evidenceOverclaiming is the most common credibility failure in STEM"The results suggest" and "is consistent with", not "proves" unless it does
Use units and significant figures consistentlyPrecision is part of the claim940 Mbps, 0.4 ms, 99.9 percent, not "very fast"

5.3 The lean paragraph

A paragraph is one unit of argument. It has four parts, usually in this order:

PartFunctionExample opening
Topic sentenceStates the claim the paragraph will support"Sixteen gigabytes is the correct RAM choice for the workstation build."
EvidenceGives the reason, data or source"The working set of Windows 11, a browser and Office exceeds 8 GB in normal use (Microsoft, 2024)."
AnalysisExplains what the evidence means for your claim"Once physical memory is exhausted the OS pages to disk, which is felt as slowness."
LinkConnects to the next paragraph or back to the question"The cost is small against a year-two rebuild, which is why the BOM carries 16 GB."

Four to eight sentences is a typical lean paragraph. One sentence is not a paragraph in academic writing (except occasionally for emphasis). Twelve sentences is usually two paragraphs that have not been separated.

The test for a lean paragraph. Cover everything except the first sentence. Can you still tell what the paragraph is about? Cover everything except the last sentence. Does the paragraph still lead somewhere? If both answers are yes, the paragraph has a shape.

5.4 Cohesion across paragraphs

Cohesion is what stops a report reading as a list of disconnected observations. Three techniques do most of the work. Signposting: tell the reader where you are going ("This section evaluates the hardware; the next section evaluates the software"). Repetition of key terms: in technical writing, repeating the exact term is better than elegant variation, because variation introduces ambiguity. Transitions used sparingly: "however", "therefore" and "in contrast" earn their place when they mark a real logical turn, and become noise when used in every paragraph.

5.5 Structure of a STEM report

SectionWhat it doesCommon failure
AbstractStates problem, method, result, conclusion in 150 to 250 wordsWritten first, before the results are known
IntroductionContext, problem, gap, aim, structureToo much general background, no gap
Literature reviewMaps the conversation and locates the gapAuthor-by-author summary, no synthesis
MethodWhat you did, in enough detail to repeat itMissing parameters, no justification for choices
ResultsThe data, presented, not interpretedInterpretation mixed into results
DiscussionWhat the results mean, limitations, implicationsRepeating the results without interpreting
ConclusionWhat you conclude and what should happen nextIntroducing new material
ReferencesThe audit trailMismatch with in-text citations
AppendicesSupporting material too long for the bodyHiding the main evidence in an appendix

5.6 Tables and figures

Every table and figure must have a number, a caption, a source line if it is not yours, and a sentence in the body that refers to it ("Table 3 shows that..."). A table that is never mentioned in the text is decoration. A table that duplicates the text word for word wastes space. The rule is: present the numbers in the table, interpret them in the text, and never do both jobs twice.

5.7 Editing pass

PassWhat you look for
1. StructureDoes each section do its job? Is the gap clear? Does the conclusion answer the introduction?
2. ParagraphDoes each paragraph have one claim? Is the topic sentence first? Is there a link?
3. SentenceAny sentence over 30 words? Any two ideas in one sentence? Any passive that hides the actor?
4. WordAny nominalisation? Any filler ("it is important to note")? Any unhedged overclaim?
5. CitationDoes every claim of fact have a source? Does every citation appear in the list?
6. ConsistencyUnits, spelling variant, terminology, tense, figure numbering

6. Plagiarism, similarity and Turnitin

6.1 What plagiarism is

Plagiarism is presenting someone else's work, words or ideas as your own. It does not require intent. Copying a paragraph and forgetting the citation is plagiarism whether or not you meant it. The offence is that the reader is misled about the origin of the work.

6.2 Types of plagiarism

TypeWhat it looks likeWhy students do it
VerbatimCopying text word for word without quotation marks and citationTime pressure; believing that changing a few words is enough
Mosaic or patchworkStitching together phrases from several sources, with citation but no real paraphraseConfusing "referenced" with "rewritten"
Inadequate paraphraseSynonym swapping while keeping the sentence structureNot knowing what paraphrasing actually is
Uncited ideaUsing someone's argument or finding without a citationBelieving that ideas are free if you did not copy the words
Self-plagiarismReusing your own earlier submitted work without disclosureNot knowing that prior submission counts
CollusionWorking with others when the task is individual, or sharing your workBelieving collaboration is always allowed
Contract cheatingPaying someone, or a service, to produce the workTime pressure; desperation
FabricationInventing data, results or referencesThe most serious category; often AI-assisted

6.3 Paraphrasing, quoting, summarising

TechniqueWhat it isWhen to use it
ParaphraseRestate the idea in your own words and sentence structure, with a citationMost of the time. This is the default.
QuoteUse the exact words in quotation marks, with page numberWhen the exact wording matters: a definition, a legal clause, a standard's precise requirement
SummariseCondense a longer passage into its main claim, with a citationWhen you need the conclusion of a long argument or a whole paper
How to paraphrase properly. Read the passage until you understand it. Close the source. Write the idea from memory in your own words, in a way that fits your argument. Then open the source and check that you have not reproduced its structure or its distinctive phrases. Add the citation. If you cannot write it from memory, you do not understand it well enough to paraphrase it yet. Synonym swapping is not paraphrasing and Turnitin will catch it.

6.4 What is common knowledge

Common knowledge is a fact that a reader in your field would accept without checking a source. It does not need a citation. In STEM, "water boils at 100 degrees Celsius at sea level" is common knowledge; "Wi-Fi 6 was standardised as 802.11ax in 2021" is arguably common knowledge in a computing report but should be cited in a general report; "this campus has 800 staff" is a fact about a specific case and needs a source. When in doubt, cite. Over-citing is a mild weakness; under-citing is misconduct.

6.5 What Turnitin actually does

Turnitin compares your text against a large database: published journal and book content, web pages, and previously submitted student papers. It reports a similarity score and highlights the matching strings. It does not decide whether you plagiarised. It flags matches for a human to review. This distinction is essential.

Turnitin doesTurnitin does not
Find strings of words that match its databaseUnderstand the meaning of your text
Report a percentage of matched textDecide whether a match is plagiarism
Show which sources match and whereDetect a well-paraphrased copy of an idea with no matching words
Exclude quotes and references if configured to do soGuarantee that your work is original

6.6 Reading the similarity score honestly

ScoreWhat it might meanWhat to do
High (say 40 percent)Could be a correctly quoted and referenced block from a standard, or a copied essayRead the report. If the matches are in quotes with citations, it may be fine. If they are in your own prose, rewrite.
Moderate (say 15 percent)Could be a few common phrases, standard terminology, or a copied paragraphCheck each match. Standard phrases and references are usually excluded.
Low (say 3 percent)Could be clean work, or a copied paragraph from a source not in the databaseDo not treat a low score as proof of originality. Turnitin only knows what it has seen.
The two myths that cost students most. Myth one: "I need to get under 10 percent or I fail." False. A high score can be innocent (quoted standards, long reference lists) and a low score can hide misconduct (a copied paragraph from a book not in the database, or AI-generated text). Myth two: "If Turnitin did not flag it, it is fine." False. Turnitin is a tool, not a verdict, and the marker reads your work.

6.7 How to reduce similarity legitimately

  1. Write from your own notes, not from the source text. Never have the source open while you draft a paraphrase.
  2. Quote only when the exact words matter, and keep quotes short.
  3. Cite everything you use, and cite it at the point of use.
  4. Reduce the size of the reference list only if it is genuinely bloated, not to game the score.
  5. Use standard terminology freely; it is not plagiarism to use the correct technical term.
  6. Do not use "synonym swap" tools or AI paraphrasers. They produce awkward text and do not fool a human marker.

6.8 The integrity of your own prior work

Reusing your own earlier assignment in a new module without disclosure is self-plagiarism. It is not always wrong to build on earlier work, but it must be disclosed: "This section draws on my earlier report for module X" with a citation. The offence is the concealment, not the reuse.

6.9 Collusion versus collaboration

Collaboration is working together to understand material. Collusion is producing submitted work together when the task is individual. If the brief says individual, discussing ideas is usually fine, but sharing text, code or files is not. When in doubt, ask the lecturer before you share, not after.

7. Ethical use of AI tools

7.1 The distinction that matters

The useful question is not "did you use AI?" but "what did you use it for, and can you defend what you submitted?" There is a real difference between using an AI tool to understand a concept and using it to produce the work you submit. The first is closer to using a textbook or a tutor. The second is closer to submitting someone else's work, and is what most academic integrity policies prohibit when they ban AI tools.

TaskTypical positionWhat to do
Asking an AI to explain a concept you do not understandUsually acceptableThen verify against a textbook or paper, and cite the source you verified, not the chatbot
Using AI to suggest search terms or a reading listUsually acceptableFind and read the actual sources
Using AI to check grammar or clarity in your own textOften acceptable, check the handbookKeep your own wording; do not accept rewrites that change your meaning
Using AI to debug code you wroteOften acceptable, check the handbookYou must be able to explain the fix
Asking AI to write your submitted proseUsually prohibitedWrite it yourself
Asking AI to generate code you submitUsually prohibited unless the brief allows itIf allowed, disclose and be able to explain every line
Asking AI for referencesDangerousAI tools fabricate plausible references. Never cite a source you have not opened.
Using AI when the brief explicitly bans itProhibitedDo not use it, even for planning

7.2 The cardinal sin: fabricated references

Large language models generate text that looks like a reference. Author names, journal titles, years, volumes and DOIs can all be invented and all look real. A fabricated reference is academic misconduct even when it was produced by accident, because the reader is misled about evidence that does not exist. This is the single most damaging AI failure in academic work, and it happens constantly.

The rule. Never put a reference in your list that you have not personally opened. Not from an AI tool, not from a friend's list, not from a citation generator you have not checked. Open the source, confirm the author, year, title, venue and pages, and only then cite it. If you cannot find it, do not cite it.

7.3 Disclosure

If your institution allows any AI use, it will usually require disclosure: what tool, for what task, and how you verified the output. A short statement in an appendix is enough. Disclosure is not a confession; it is part of the integrity record, and it protects you if a marker notices AI-like phrasing in your work. Undisclosed use is what turns a permitted use into misconduct.

7.4 AI detectors

AI detectors are unreliable in both directions. They produce false positives (flagging human writing, especially from non-native speakers, as AI) and false negatives (missing AI-generated text). Passing a detector is not evidence of integrity, and failing one is not proof of misconduct. Do not build a strategy around beating a detector. Build a strategy around being able to defend every sentence in a viva.

7.5 The viva test

Imagine a marker asking you to explain any sentence in your submission, justify any design choice, and modify any part of your code. If you can, the work is yours in the sense that matters. If you cannot, no detector result will save you, because the marker does not need a detector to notice that you cannot explain your own report.

The three questions before you submit. Can I explain every sentence? Can I defend every design decision? Can I modify this work if asked? If the answer to any of these is no, the problem is not the detector. Fix the work.

7.6 Using AI without losing the skill

The reason institutions restrict AI is not suspicion for its own sake. The skills you are being assessed on, reading, synthesising, arguing, designing, debugging, are built by doing them. If AI does the doing, you do not build the skill. The students who get the most out of AI use it as a tutor and a sparring partner, not as a ghostwriter. Ask it to explain a concept, to quiz you, to point out weaknesses in an argument you wrote, to suggest what you might have missed. Then do the work yourself.

8. Checklists, glossary and self-test

8.1 Before you submit: the integrity checklist

  1. Every claim of fact has a citation, and every citation is in the reference list.
  2. Every reference in the list is a source I have personally opened and verified.
  3. No reference was generated by an AI tool without verification.
  4. Every quotation is in quotation marks with a page number.
  5. Every paraphrase is in my own words and sentence structure, with a citation.
  6. I have not reused my own earlier submitted work without disclosure.
  7. I have not shared text or files when the task was individual.
  8. I can explain every sentence, every design decision and every line of code.
  9. I have run the similarity check, read the report, and resolved every match that is in my own prose.
  10. I have checked the module handbook for any AI-specific rule and complied with it.
  11. I have completed and signed the declaration of originality if one is required.

8.2 Before you submit: the writing checklist

  1. The introduction states the gap, and the conclusion answers the introduction.
  2. Every paragraph has one claim and a topic sentence.
  3. Average sentence length is under 25 words.
  4. Acronyms are defined at first use and used consistently.
  5. Claims are hedged to match the evidence.
  6. Units and significant figures are consistent.
  7. Every table and figure is numbered, captioned, sourced and referred to in the text.
  8. The reference list is alphabetical, in one style, with hanging indents.
  9. No source is cited only because it is old, and none only because it is new.
  10. The document has been read aloud once.
